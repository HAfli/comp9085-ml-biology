**The three things students must leave with**

1. A tree asks the question whose answer is least predictable, and entropy is
   how "least predictable" is measured.
2. `DecisionTreeClassifier` is CART-style, not ID3, and the difference is
   visible in any tree you draw.
3. A model can be interpretable and still be wrong.

### Slide 1 — Decision trees

Open with the question, not the algorithm: a pathologist has measured a
breast-mass biopsy and wants to know whether it is malignant. By the end of
the two hours the room will have built a model that answers it, looked inside
the model, and found the reasons not to trust it yet.

### Slide 2 — This lecture

Say that Acts 1 to 3 are the same material as last year, tightened. Acts 4 to
6 are new and they are where the module differs from a textbook treatment: the
tree on the screen in Act 4 is fitted to real data in front of them, and in
Act 6 they put a case into it themselves.

### Slide 3 — Where Week 2 sits

Students ask every year why we spend a whole week on one of the simplest
models. The answer: it is the only model in the module you can read
completely, so every question about trust can be asked here concretely before
the models get opaque.


## Act 1 — Why trees?

### Slide 4 — Why trees?

_Section divider. Say the act title and move on._

### Slide 5 — The biological problem

Ask where the numbers come from before you say. Someone always answers 'a
scan'; the real answer is that a human traced the nuclei, which is the first
place error enters. REAL DATA: UCI Breast Cancer Wisconsin (Diagnostic),
loaded in the lab with sklearn.datasets.load_breast_cancer.

### Slide 6 — The features are already meaningful numbers.

This is the course argument arriving early. Foundation models earn their place
when the raw input is a sequence, an image or text and nobody has engineered
features for it. Here somebody has. Week 7 gives them the other case.

### Slide 7 — Anyone for a game of Guess Who?

Original slides 3 and 4. Play it properly: two volunteers, one picks a
character, the other asks yes/no questions from the feature list only. Then
put the two opening questions to the room and take a vote before showing the
figure. The original speaker note asks for a poll here; do that if the room is
large.

### Slide 8 — The four characters

Leave this up while they argue. The glasses question splits 1 / 3; the man
question splits 2 / 2. That asymmetry is the whole idea, and they can see it
in the table before any mathematics.

### Slide 9 — So which question should we start with?

Original slide 9, close to verbatim. The sentence that matters is the last
one: informativeness is about the sizes of the resulting sets and how likely
each answer is, not about the answer itself.

### Slide 10 — Find the features whose answers are least predictable, and ask about those first.

Original slide 10. If a student remembers one sentence from Act 1, this is it.
It also previews why the maths is about probabilities rather than about the
features themselves.

### Slide 11 — Root, interior nodes, leaves

Original slides 13 to 16 were four animation states of one picture. Name the
three parts and move on: the vocabulary matters, the animation did not.

### Slide 12 — One query travelling through the tree

Original slide 18, same query values. Walk it on the board with your finger.
Note that Blood Pressure is never tested: the tree did not need it for this
case. Students find that surprising and it is worth ten seconds. In Act 4 we
do exactly this on a real held-out biopsy.

### Slide 13 — But we can build many trees

Original slides 19 to 22. Take a vote, as the original note asks. Almost
everyone picks (a), and almost nobody can say why without prompting. Let the
silence sit before the next slide.

### Slide 14 — Inductive bias

Original slides 23 and 24. The last line is the one worth labouring: without a
preference, any tree that fits the training data is as good as any other, and
there are very many of them. The bias is not a flaw, it is what makes learning
possible at all.

### Slide 15 — How do we create simple trees?

Original slide 25. This is the hinge from Act 1 into Act 2, and it is worth
pausing on the word 'pure': a set is pure when everything in it has the same
label, which is exactly when you no longer need to ask anything.


## Act 2 — How does a tree choose?

### Slide 16 — How does a tree choose?

_Section divider. Say the act title and move on._

### Slide 17 — Shannon's entropy

Original slides 26 and 28. Do not rush the minus sign: probabilities are below
1, their logs are negative, and the minus makes entropy positive. Ask the room
what H is for a set where every case is malignant, before you show the
glossary.

### Slide 18 — Pure and mixed nodes, on real cases

Point at the last row: entropy zero means that if you drew a case from that
leaf at random you would know its label without looking. That is what the
algorithm is driving towards, and it is also, as Act 5 shows, exactly what it
can overdo.

### Slide 19 — Worked example: heart-disease risk

The original worked example runs on 25 instances but prints only these 13, so
the figures in the old deck cannot be verified from the slide. Recomputing on
the printed rows fixes that. The conclusion does not change: Age still wins
the root. See docs/WEEK02_SOURCE_MAPPING.md for both sets of numbers.

### Slide 20 — One split, worked in full

Original slides 35 to 44, condensed. Do this one on the board as well as on
the slide. The weighting is the step students skip: a very pure partition
holding two patients is worth less than a slightly less pure one holding
eleven.

### Slide 21 — All four features, compared

Original slides 38 to 57 worked through each feature in turn over sixteen
slides. The arithmetic is identical each time, so one worked example and this
table teach it. Ask which row wins before revealing: Age, as in the original
deck. Blood pressure barely helps, which is worth a sentence, because a
feature can be clinically important and still be a poor first question.

### Slide 22 — Information gain

Original slides 55 to 57, including the original's own remark that you can
just use entropy directly. Both forms appear in the literature and in the lab,
so name both.

### Slide 23 — Workshop: which feature belongs at the root?

Original slides 58 and 59, the in-class workshop, with the lecturer's own
table. Categorical features make the arithmetic easy: no thresholds to search.
Walk round while they work; the usual error is forgetting to weight the
partitions by size. Reveal on the next slide.

### Slide 24 — Workshop: the answer

Answer: Smoker, gain 0.459 bits. Two things to draw out. First, Smoker does
not split these six perfectly: patient 5 smokes and is low risk, so the
partition still carries 0.811 bits. Students expect a clean answer and do not
get one, which is realistic. Second, Obese gains almost nothing here, which
surprises people, and is a good moment to say that a feature can matter
clinically and still be a poor question to ask first.


## Act 3 — From idea to algorithm

### Slide 25 — From idea to algorithm

_Section divider. Say the act title and move on._

### Slide 26 — The ID3 algorithm

Original slides 60 and 61, close to verbatim. The word to stress is
'recursively': the same procedure applies to each partition, which is why one
paragraph describes a tree of any size. The exclusion of a used feature is an
ID3 property, and the next-but-one slide explains why scikit-learn does not do
it.

### Slide 27 — Stopping rules

Original slide 76. Ask which of the three fires most often in practice: the
first. The third is the awkward one and it is worth asking why an empty
partition can arise at all.

### Slide 28 — Applying it to the heart-disease set

Original slides 62 to 74 walked every partition. That is valuable the first
time you meet it and tedious the second; the lab reproduces it
computationally. If the room is following well, do one level of the recursion
live on the board.

### Slide 29 — Using the tree

Original slide 75, same query. The third bullet is the one to dwell on. Ask:
on what grounds should we believe the answer? Nobody has a good answer yet,
which is the right state to be in going into Act 5.

### Slide 30 — Handling continuous features

Original slides 77 to 81. The original deck raises this honestly and then says
the mean happened to win on its data. On WDBC it does not, and the next slide
shows by how much. Do not let anyone leave thinking the threshold is the mean.

### Slide 31 — Every candidate threshold, evaluated

This slide is new and it settles the question original slide 79 leaves open.
The mean is a reasonable guess and it is 0.069 bits worse here. Ask why the
curve is so ragged: each candidate moves one patient across the boundary, so
the gain jumps whenever that patient's label differs from its neighbour's.

### Slide 32 — ID3 is not what scikit-learn runs

This slide is new and it matters for the lab and for both projects. Saying
'sklearn implements ID3' is wrong and students repeat it in reports. We set
criterion='entropy' throughout the module so the splitting criterion matches
the lecture, but the tree structure is still CART: strictly binary, and
features can recur down a path. Point out that both of those differences are
visible in the tree on the next slide.


## Act 4 — Real biological data

### Slide 33 — Real biological data

_Section divider. Say the act title and move on._

### Slide 34 — The experiment, stated before it is run

The baseline is the line everyone forgets. 62.7 per cent is what you get for
free by always saying benign, so any model has to beat that before it has done
anything at all. Ask the room to commit to a guess for the tree's accuracy
before you show it.

### Slide 35 — The actual fitted tree

Give them thirty seconds of silence to read it before you say anything. Then:
the root is worst perimeter at 112.8, gaining 0.585 bits, which is the number
Act 2 was about. Note that it is binary everywhere and that a feature recurs
on a path, both CART properties from the previous slide. The website renders
this same exported tree and lets them change the depth.

### Slide 36 — The root split, checked by hand

The third glossary line is the important one and it recurs in Act 6. The tree
has found a real statistical regularity in 398 biopsies from one institution
in the 1990s. That is not the same as a clinical decision rule, and students
write it up as though it were.

### Slide 37 — Three candidate splits at the root

This is the Guess Who slide again, on real data, and it is worth saying so
explicitly: glasses versus is-it-a-man has become mean texture versus worst
perimeter. Same question, same measure, forty minutes apart.

### Slide 38 — One real held-out case

Ask the question before showing the next slide: why did this case reach this
leaf? The answer is that two tests were enough; the other 28 features were
never consulted. Students find that unsettling, correctly.

### Slide 39 — The path, on the tree

Call this the decision path, not the reasoning and not the explanation. It is
the sequence of learned splits this input followed. It does not establish
causality and it is not a clinical justification. That wording appears on the
website and in the lab too, deliberately.


## Act 5 — Can we trust the tree?

### Slide 40 — Can we trust the tree?

_Section divider. Say the act title and move on._

### Slide 41 — Overfitting

Original slide 82, verbatim in the first two bullets. A tree that is perfect
on its training data has memorised it: there is a leaf for every awkward case.
Ask whether 94.2 per cent held-out is actually bad. It is not, and that is the
interesting part: overfitting here costs accuracy but does not destroy it.
What it destroys is the tree's readability.

### Slide 42 — Depth against performance, measured

Walk left to right. At depth 1 the tree is a single question and already beats
the 62.7 per cent baseline. The curves separate from about depth 5; after that
the training curve runs to 100 per cent while the held-out curve is flat,
dipping at depth 6 and recovering. Point out that dip: it is one case in 171,
which is what noise looks like, and it is a good reason not to read a single
accuracy to three decimal places. Ask what they would report as this model's
accuracy, take the answer 94.2 per cent, and then break it on the next slide
but one.

### Slide 43 — Where the errors are

This is the slide that changes how they read accuracy. 14 malignant cases were
called benign. In a screening context that is the error that matters, and a
model chosen on accuracy alone will happily trade it away. Week 4 gives them
sensitivity and specificity; Week 12 gives them the rest.

### Slide 44 — Pruning

Original slides 83 to 85. Both approaches are in the lab. The honest framing:
pre-pruning is cheap and blunt, post-pruning is principled and needs data you
have to set aside. Neither is free.

### Slide 45 — Pruning, measured on the same split

The shape is the point: there is a middle, and you cannot find it by looking
at the training curve. Then ask the awkward question, which the next slide
answers: we just picked ccp_alpha by looking at the held-out set. What is
wrong with that?

### Slide 46 — We just chose a hyperparameter by looking at the test set. That number is no longer an honest estimate.

This is the sentence Week 3 is built on and the most common error in both
projects. Say plainly that the 94.2 per cent on the earlier slide is
optimistic for exactly this reason, and that the honest protocol is in Task 8
of the lab.

### Slide 47 — Decision trees: advantages and disadvantages

Original slide 88, verbatim. Connect the first advantage to Act 6:
interpretability is the reason this model can be deployed and interrogated in
a lecture theatre, and it is not the same thing as being right.

### Slide 48 — A model can be interpretable and still be wrong.

The sentence to leave them with from Act 5. It is also the bridge into the
deployment sequence: they are about to see the model being used, and this is
the frame to see it through.


## Act 6 — From model to application

### Slide 49 — From model to application

_Section divider. Say the act title and move on._

### Slide 50 — What deployment actually means

The gap between the two rows is where most harm in applied ML happens: the
person in the last box did not see the split, does not know the baseline, and
has no idea the training data came from one institution. Demonstrate the
interface for about five minutes. Load the malignant example, show the path,
then raise worst perimeter slightly and watch the prediction flip at the
threshold. Then type in something absurd, 900 for worst perimeter, and show
the out-of-range warning rather than a confident answer. Students remember the
second demonstration longer than the first.

### Slide 51 — Try this interactively

Three activities on one page, all driven by the same exported tree. Run the
builder in the lecture: ask for a prediction before you move the depth slider,
because most of the room expects deeper to be better, and watching the held-
out number fall while the training number rises does more work than the curve
on the earlier slide did. Use the entropy visualiser back in Act 2 instead if
the room struggled with the weighting: dragging the threshold and seeing the
weighted average move is the fastest way to make it concrete.

### Slide 52 — Decision path is not an explanation

Worth being firm here. Interpretable models invite the slide from 'this is
what it did' to 'this is why it is true', and the language does the sliding.
If a student writes 'the model reasoned that...' in a project, this is the
slide to point at.

### Slide 53 — Should we trust this result?

Put this to the room as eight questions and take answers. For our tree:
validated on a held-out split, yes; test set kept clean, no, we peeked when
choosing depth; representative, unknown and probably not, it is one
institution in the 1990s; external validation, none; shift, very likely;
consequences, asymmetric and we optimised as though they were not. Week 12 is
this slide expanded into two hours.

### Slide 54 — Training a model is not the end of machine learning.

The sentence of the week. It is also, almost word for word, the marking
criterion for both projects.

### Slide 55 — Exit ticket, and the laboratory

Collect the exit tickets if the room is small enough; the third question is
the one that separates students who have understood the week from students who
have followed it. The lab notebook and its HTML version are on the Week 2
page.

### Slide 56 — Summary

Original slide 87 covered the first and third lines. The rest are the week's

## Where the website differs, and why that is worth a sentence

The slides were produced by scikit-learn. The website refits a tree **in the
browser** with the module's own implementation in `js/ml.js`, using its own
stratified splitter. The two therefore see slightly different training sets and
report slightly different thresholds — around 112.8 on the slides, around 115.4
on the site, both on the same root feature.

Say this out loud if a student notices. It is a better teaching moment than a
coincidence would have been: two honest implementations, same data, same
criterion, same winning feature, slightly different threshold, because the split
differs. That is what sampling variability looks like, and it is the reason a
single number with no protocol attached means very little.

## Answers to the questions on the slides

- **Slide 7, which opening question?** "Is it a man?" The glasses question
  splits 1/3, entropy 0.811 bits. The man question splits 2/2, entropy
  1.000 bits. The more balanced split is the more informative question.
- **Slide 14, the query.** Age 62 > 50, Chest Pain 4 > 2.5 → high risk. Blood
  Pressure is never tested.
- **Slide 25, which feature at the root?** Age, remainder 0.250, gain
  0.530 bits.
- **Slide 30, the workshop.** Smoker, gain 0.459 bits. It does **not** split
  the six perfectly: patient 5 smokes and is low risk, so that partition still
  carries 0.811 bits. Students expect a clean answer and should not get one.
- **Slide 32, which stopping rule fires most?** The first: all instances share
  a classification.
- **Slide 37, why is the threshold curve ragged?** Each candidate moves one
  patient across the boundary, so the gain jumps whenever that patient's label
  differs from its neighbour's.
- **Slide 44, is 94.2 per cent held out actually bad?** No, and that is the
  interesting part. Overfitting here costs a little accuracy and destroys the
  tree's readability: 16 leaves instead of 6.
- **Slide 49, the eight trust questions.** Validated on a held-out split, yes.
  Test set kept clean, no — we chose the depth by looking at it. Representative,
  unknown and probably not. External validation, none. Shift, very likely.
  Features, they need a person to trace nuclei. Error costs, asymmetric and we
  optimised as though they were not. Clinically validated, no.

## Common misconceptions, and what to say

| They say | Say back |
|---|---|
| "scikit-learn implements ID3" | It is CART-style. Binary splits everywhere, and a feature can recur down a path. Both are visible in the tree on slide 35. |
| "The threshold is the mean" | Slide 37 measures it: 374 candidates evaluated, the chosen one is 112.8 and the mean would have cost 0.069 bits. |
| "Deeper is more accurate" | On the training set, always. On held-out data it peaks at depth 5 here and falls after. |
| "The tree explains the diagnosis" | It reports the decision path: the sequence of learned splits this input followed. It does not establish causality. |
| "91.8 per cent accuracy, so it works" | Against a 62.7 per cent baseline, on 171 cases from one institution in the 1990s, with 14 missed malignancies. Which of those did the accuracy tell you? |
| "It's interpretable, so it's safe" | You can read every rule. That tells you what it does, not whether what it does is right. |
